The ones I'd prioritize

1. Lecce - If you like architecture and restaurants
The historic center is packed with ornate Baroque buildings, churches and Roman remains. It's also a particularly useful base for exploring Salento.

2. Ostuni - For the classic Puglia look
The Città Bianca is built around a hilltop historic center of whitewashed buildings and winding lanes. It's especially atmospheric around sunset.

3. Monopoli - For a combination of town + sea
A lovely historic port, with narrow streets, old churches, defensive walls and beaches/coves nearby. It's also a practical base for exploring the central coast.

4. Polignano a Mare - For scenery
This is the postcard Puglia: an old town perched above the Adriatic, with the famous Lama Monachile cove directly below it. It's spectacular, though relatively small.

5. Alberobello - For something you won't see elsewhere
The town's distinctive trulli-stone buildings with conical roofs-are the main attraction. It's one of the most recognizable places in Puglia and is particularly easy to combine with Ostuni and the other Valle d'Itria towns.

6. Bari - For food, urban atmosphere and convenience
Bari Vecchia has narrow medieval streets, the Basilica of San Nicola, the cathedral and Norman-Swabian Castle. Bari is also one of the region's main transport hubs, making it useful if you don't want to rent a car.

If you have 7 days

I'd structure it roughly as:

Bari → Polignano → Monopoli → Alberobello/Locorotondo → Ostuni → Lecce → Otranto

That gives you a good balance of cities + villages + architecture + coastline, rather than spending the whole trip moving between similar beach towns. Current travel guides also group these naturally into the central Adriatic, Valle d'Itria and Salento areas.

One important distinction: Alberobello, Polignano and Ostuni are often called "cities" casually, but they're really towns/small historic settlements. That's actually part of what makes Puglia special.

The best cities to visit in Puglia are Alberobello, Polignano a Mare, Lecce, Ostuni, Bari, and Monopoli — each offering a unique mix of history, architecture, and coastal beauty.

🏛 Cultural & Historical Highlights

  • Alberobello: The Trulli, Rione Monti district and Trullo Sovrano museum showcase Puglia’s most iconic architecture.
  • Lecce: Rich in Baroque art, with ornate facades like Basilica di Santa Croce and Piazza del Duomo.
  • Ostuni: Its labyrinth of whitewashed alleys and hilltop cathedral make it one of Italy’s most photogenic towns.

🌊 Coastal & Natural Attractions

  • Polignano a Mare: Lama Monachile beach is a postcard‑perfect cove framed by cliffs.
  • Monopoli: The seaside fortress Castello Carlo V offers sweeping Adriatic views.
  • Castellana Grotte: The Grotte di Castellana cave system is one of Italy’s most impressive karst formations.

⚠️ Tips & Considerations

  • Seasonality: Summer months (July–August) bring crowds and higher prices; spring and autumn are quieter and more affordable.
  • Transport: Puglia’s towns are best explored by car, as public transport between smaller cities can be limited.
  • Local cuisine: Each city has specialties — orecchiette pasta in Bari, seafood in Polignano, and rustic olive oil dishes in Ostuni.
  • Hidden gems: Smaller towns like Martina Franca and Cisternino are worth visiting for authentic local life.

✅ Recommendation

If you want a balanced itinerary, spend 2–3 days in Lecce for culture, 1–2 days in Alberobello for trulli, and several days along the coast in Polignano a Mare and Monopoli. This mix gives you history, architecture, and seaside relaxation — the essence of Puglia.

Gemini

Puglia , the sun-drenched "heel" of Italy’s boot, is a sprawling region defined by centuries-old olive groves, dramatic coastlines, and distinct stone architecture. Because the region spans over 250 miles from north to south, the towns vary significantly in personality.

The absolute best cities and towns to visit in Puglia are grouped below by their unique geographic charm:

🌊 The Dramatic Coast (Central Adriatic)

These towns are located just south of the regional capital of Bari and are ideal if you want a mix of vibrant seaside energy and historic centers.

Polignano a Mare: Famous for its white-pebble beach, Lama Monachile, tucked dramatically between soaring limestone cliffs. It features a maze of poetic alleys opening up onto terraces 30 meters above the turquoise sea.

Monopoli: Just down the road from Polignano, Monopoli is a beautifully preserved medieval fishing village. It feels slightly more local and lived-in, featuring a historic harbor with blue wooden boats and a 16th-century seaside castle.

Trani: Located north of Bari, this hidden gem is often missed by tourists. It is famous for its elegant, white-stone Norman cathedral that sits directly on the water's edge, reflecting against the Adriatic Sea.

🏡 The Fairytale Countryside (Valle d'Itria)

Moving inland, the landscape changes to rolling hills and vineyards. This area is world-renowned for its distinct whitewashed architecture.

Alberobello: A mandatory stop to witness the Trulli, Rione Monti, a UNESCO World Heritage site consisting of over 1,000 stone huts with conical roofs. It is highly touristy, so it is best visited early in the morning or late evening.

Ostuni ("La Città Bianca"): A striking, labyrinthine city completely covered in white lime wash, perched high on a hill overlooking miles of olive groves extending to the sea.

Locorotondo & Martina Franca: Locorotondo is a quiet, circular, hilltop town known for its flower-adorned balconies and calm vibe. Neighboring Martina Franca contrasts sharply with grand, elegant Baroque palaces and stately churches.

🏛️ The Baroque South (Salento Peninsula)

The southern "heel" of Puglia offers the region's absolute best sandy beaches alongside rich, historic centers.

Lecce: Known as the "Florence of the South," Lecce is the cultural capital of the southern half. It is completely built out of soft, golden "Lecce stone" carved into intricate Baroque masterpieces. It boasts incredible dining and a buzzing nightlife.

Otranto: Located on the eastern coast, this historic port features crystal-clear swimming waters right up to its castle walls. Its 12th-century cathedral houses an unmissable, massive floor mosaic.

Gallipoli: Perched on the western, Ionian Sea coast, Gallipoli’s historic center sits on a fortified limestone island connected to the mainland by a 17th-century bridge. It is famous for fresh seafood markets, historic alleys, and spectacular sunsets over the water.

💡 Pro-Tips for Your Trip

Transportation: Renting a car is highly recommended if you want to explore the countryside and coast freely. While Bari, Polignano, Monopoli, and Lecce are well-connected by trains, the smaller countryside villages are difficult to reach with public transport.

Where to Base Yourself: A classic mistake is changing hotels every night. Instead, pick a central coastal town like Monopoli or an inland farmhouse (masseria) near Ostuni for the northern/central part of your trip, then move down to Lecce or Otranto to explore the south.

Grok · grok-3

The best cities and towns to visit in Puglia (Apulia), the heel of Italy’s boot, combine dramatic coastal scenery, distinctive architecture (especially trulli and Baroque), whitewashed hill towns, excellent food, and history.

Here are the standout ones, based on frequent recommendations from travel guides:

Top Highlights

  • Lecce — Often called the “Florence of the South.” Its historic center is a showcase of ornate Baroque architecture in golden pietra leccese stone. Don’t miss the Basilica di Santa Croce, the Duomo and Piazza del Duomo, the Roman amphitheatre, and the lively university-town atmosphere. An excellent base for the Salento peninsula.

  • Alberobello — The most iconic stop for trulli (traditional conical-roofed limestone houses). This UNESCO World Heritage site has over a thousand of them clustered together, creating a fairy-tale landscape. It can get very crowded in high season, so visit early or late in the day.

  • Ostuni (“the White City”) — A striking hilltop town of whitewashed buildings cascading down the hillside, with views over olive groves toward the Adriatic. The historic center is a maze of narrow lanes, perfect for wandering. Great for combining with the surrounding Valle d’Itria.

  • Polignano a Mare — Dramatically perched on limestone cliffs above turquoise water. The old town is atmospheric, and the famous cove beach (Lama Monachile) sits right below the cliffs. Excellent for views, swimming, and photos.

  • Bari — Puglia’s lively capital and main transport hub. The old town (Bari Vecchia) is atmospheric with laundry-strung alleys, the Basilica di San Nicola (home to the relics of St. Nicholas), street food (especially orecchiette pasta made by local nonnas), and a pleasant seafront. Often underestimated but worth at least a day.

Other Excellent Choices

  • Otranto — Historic coastal town with a famous Romanesque cathedral (featuring remarkable mosaics and a chapel of skulls from a 1480 Ottoman siege). It has a pleasant old quarter, beaches, and a strategic position near Italy’s easternmost point.
  • Gallipoli — A fortified old town on a small island connected by a bridge, known for its sea walls, beaches, seafood, and lively summer atmosphere on the Ionian coast.
  • Monopoli — A more authentic working harbor town with a castle, old center, fresh seafood, and nearby beaches. Often preferred by those seeking less-touristy coastal vibes than Polignano.
  • Trani — Elegant coastal city nicknamed the “Pearl of Puglia,” famous for its beautiful seaside cathedral and harbor.
  • Valle d’Itria towns (especially Locorotondo, Cisternino, and Martina Franca) — Charming whitewashed or Baroque villages amid olive groves, vineyards, and trulli countryside. These feel more local and less crowded than Alberobello or Ostuni.

Quick Planning Notes

Puglia is long and narrow, so many visitors focus on one area (Valle d’Itria + Adriatic coast around Bari/Polignano/Monopoli, or the southern Salento around Lecce/Otranto/Gallipoli) rather than trying to cover everything. A car is very useful for flexibility between towns.

Summer (especially July–August) brings heat and crowds to the most popular spots; shoulder seasons (May–June or September–October) are often ideal. The region is also known for outstanding food—orecchiette, fresh seafood, burrata, olive oil, and local wines.

These places offer a mix of iconic sights, atmosphere, and authentic southern Italian character.
